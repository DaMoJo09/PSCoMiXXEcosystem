01
📷 Week 01 · Capture
SHOOT YOUR
SCENE

Students learn to see their environment as a set. Using nothing but their phone, they discover how angle, lighting, and framing transform ordinary spaces into dramatic backdrops for their stories.

5 SessionsMon–Fri, 55 min each
PlatformPhone Camera + pscomixx.com
Deliverable5-shot Scene Collection
Skill LevelBeginner — No prior experience
📷
Phone Camera → pscomixx.com

No special equipment needed. Every phone is a production camera. The classroom, hallway, yard, or street is the set.

Learning Objectives

  • Apply the rule of thirds to phone photography
  • Identify dramatic angles: low, high, Dutch tilt
  • Use natural and artificial light intentionally
  • Select backgrounds that support mood and story
  • Upload and organize assets in pscomixx.com

Teacher Setup Notes

  • Pre-load pscomixx.com on student devices — verify login
  • Scout 3–4 locations on campus students can shoot freely
  • Print or display the "7 Shot Types" reference sheet (Session 1)
  • Have students enable gridlines on phone cameras (rule of thirds)
  • Pair students for peer photography if needed (one shoots, one poses)

01.1 See Like a Director — 7 Shot Types Intro LECTURE + DEMO 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Open with 3 side-by-side photo pairs: same subject, completely different angles. Ask: "Which one tells a better story? Why?" Introduce the 7 shot types: Extreme Wide, Wide, Medium, Close-Up, Extreme Close-Up, Low Angle, High Angle. Students take 7 test shots of the same object in the room using each type.

Timing (Teacher View)
0:00Hook — 3 photo comparison pairs projected (5 min)
0:05Direct instruction — 7 shot types with examples (15 min)
0:20Demo — Teacher shoots 7 shots of same object live (10 min)
0:30Student practice — Shoot 7 shots of assigned object (20 min)
0:50Gallery share — 3 volunteers show best shot + explain why (5 min)
Student Activity

Pick any object in the room. Shoot it 7 times — once for each shot type. Upload your favorites to pscomixx.com. In your creation log, write one sentence about which angle felt the most dramatic and why.

THIS ISN'T JUST A PHOTO. IT'S YOUR BACKGROUND.
Resources
  • 7 Shot Types reference card (printable — included below)
  • pscomixx.com upload guide
  • Camera gridline enable instructions (iOS + Android)
01.2 Light Is Everything — Natural vs. Controlled Lighting STUDIO PRACTICE 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Students explore how light direction changes mood. Using window light, overhead light, and phone flashlights as tools, they shoot the same scene three different ways. Key vocabulary: backlight, sidelight, flat light, dramatic shadow.

Student Activity

Find a spot in the school with interesting natural light (window, doorway, shadow pattern). Shoot your scene THREE times: with light behind your subject, with light to the side, and with flat overhead light. Upload all three. Label them in pscomixx.com. Which version feels like a thriller? Which feels like daytime TV?

Timing (Teacher View)
0:00Opening comparison — same scene in 3 lighting conditions (5 min)
0:05Vocabulary intro — backlight, sidelight, flat light (10 min)
0:15Teacher demo — shoot scene in 3 conditions live (10 min)
0:25Student shoot — 3 lighting versions of their chosen scene (25 min)
0:50Pair share — swap phones, explain your favorite (5 min)
01.3 Texture Hunting — Walls, Floors, Sky & Shadow LOCATION SHOOT 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Textures make scenes feel real. Students go on a "texture hunt" around campus — cracked asphalt, brick, chain link, sunlight through leaves. These become background plates for future compositing. Students collect a minimum of 8 texture shots categorized by mood: Gritty, Clean, Mysterious, Natural.

Student Activity

You're building a texture library. Walk your campus and shoot 8 textures — at least 2 from each mood category: GRITTY (asphalt, concrete, rust), CLEAN (smooth walls, tiles, glass), MYSTERIOUS (shadows, dim corners, patterns), NATURAL (plants, sky, ground). Upload and tag them in pscomixx.com by mood. These become YOUR personal scene backgrounds.

01.4 Scene Design — Choosing Your World PLANNING + SHOOT 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Students begin pre-production planning for their final comic concept. They choose a "world" (post-apocalyptic, futuristic city, supernatural small town, etc.) and shoot scenes that match. This is the first day students use their Shot List template — a planning tool they'll carry through the course.

Student Activity

Decide on your comic's world. Pick 3 scenes that exist on campus that COULD become part of your world with FX. Fill out your Shot List: World name, 5 scenes you'll shoot, time of day for each, mood for each. Then go shoot your 5 scenes. Remember: you're looking for the angle, not the perfect location — the FX Studio will do the rest.

🎭
Phone Camera → pscomixx.com Cutout Tool

You — or your friends, family, even pets — become the cast. Blank walls and soft light make cutouts clean.

Learning Objectives

  • Direct a subject into expressive, story-driven poses
  • Identify optimal camera distance for character cutouts
  • Use blank backgrounds or greenscreen for clean isolation
  • Apply the CoMiXX cutout tool to extract character from background
  • Develop a personal character with a consistent visual identity

Teacher Setup Notes

  • Designate a character shoot zone — plain wall or hang butcher paper
  • Green or blue fabric (or large sheet of paper) for greenscreen option
  • Confirm pscomixx.com cutout tool works on student devices
  • Print the "8 Hero Poses" reference sheet for students
  • Discuss consent — students shoot each other, must agree to be photographed

02.1 The 8 Hero Poses — Reading Body Language in Comics LECTURE + PRACTICE 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Study how comic artists convey emotion through body posture and gesture. Students analyze reference panels from different comic styles. They then practice directing each other into 8 key poses: Action, Reaction, Threat, Fear, Confidence, Defeat, Discovery, Celebration.

Student Activity

Using the 8 Hero Poses sheet, take turns as director and subject. Your subject must hit each pose — you shoot it. Then switch. Upload your 8 shots. Which pose felt most natural? Which was hardest to make "readable"?

YOU ARE NOW PART OF THE SCENE.
02.2 Clean Cutouts — The Art of the Isolated Character TOOL LAB 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Introduction to the pscomixx.com cutout tool. Students learn what makes a photo "cuttable" — distance from background, contrast, soft edges. They practice cutting out their best 3 character shots and placing them on a solid color to evaluate quality.

Student Activity

Open pscomixx.com. Upload your 3 best character shots. Use the cutout tool on each. Place your cutout on a red background and zoom in — are there fuzzy edges? Halos? Fix them using the eraser refinement. Save your cleanest cutout. That's your character sprite.

02.3 Costume, Props & Context — Building Visual Character CREATIVE SHOOT 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Students build visual character identity using what's available — a jacket, backpack, hat, sunglasses, pen, phone. Props define characters instantly in comics. Students shoot their character 4 times with different "costume states" to represent emotional or situational shifts.

Student Activity

Your character needs a look. Using only what's in your bag or around the classroom, build 2 different "states" for your character — one for regular life, one for their story-moment. Shoot both. Cut them out. Name your character in your creation log. Give them one line of dialogue that tells us who they are.

02.4 Character Meets Scene — First Composite PRODUCTION LAB 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Students drop their character cutout onto one of their Week 1 scene backgrounds in pscomixx.com. First composite! Key skills: scaling, positioning, shadow awareness, perspective matching. Students discover which scenes and characters naturally "fit" and which feel off.

Student Activity

Open pscomixx.com. Load your best background from Week 1. Drop in your character cutout. Scale it to fit the perspective — does your character look like they BELONG in this world? Adjust until they do. Save your first composite. This is your first panel.

YOUR BACKYARD IS NOW A BATTLE SCENE.
02.5 Character Sheet Review + Story Pitch CRITIQUE + PITCH 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Students present their character and share a 60-second story pitch: Who is this character? What world do they live in? What problem do they face? Class provides warm feedback. Teacher connects character design to comics industry practice (model sheets, turnaround refs).

CHARACTER SHEET — 8 POSES

You now have a character — a real photo of a real person — cut out, posed, and placed in your world. This is your cast sheet.

  • 8 character poses — uploaded and cut out in pscomixx.com
  • First composite — character placed in 1 scene background
  • Character name + 1-line bio in creation log
  • 60-second story pitch delivered (or recorded)

03.1 FX Studio Orientation — Layers, Controls & First Filter TOOL INTRO 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Full walkthrough of pscomixx.online FX Studio. Students learn the layer stack: background plate → character cutout → FX overlays → text/graphics. They apply their first filter (comic halftone or ink wash) to a test composite and save.

Student Activity

Open pscomixx.online. Load your best Week 1 background as your base. Add your Week 2 character cutout. Apply ONE filter — try "Ink Wash" or "Halftone Grit." Adjust opacity until it looks like a printed comic panel. Export and save to your project folder. You just made your first real comic panel.

YOUR YARD ISN'T YOUR YARD ANYMORE. IT'S A SCENE.
03.2 Atmosphere Lab — Smoke, Rain, Glow & Distress FX FX LAB 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Students explore the atmospheric FX suite in pscomixx.online: rain, smoke, dust particles, glitch lines, glow halos, torn paper edge. They stack 2–3 FX layers on their composites, discovering how each combination changes mood.

Student Activity

Pick one of your composites from yesterday. Stack THREE atmospheric effects — one weather effect (rain/smoke), one texture effect (dust/grain), one light effect (glow/flare). Export it. Then make a second version with totally different FX. Which version fits your world? Add both to your project folder with labels.

03.3 Color & Tone — Setting the Mood of Your World COLOR THEORY 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Color grading as storytelling. Students learn how to use the tone/color controls in pscomixx.online to shift their scene into different emotional registers. Key example: the same scene — desaturated cyan tint = horror / warm orange = western / high-contrast B&W = noir.

Student Activity

Take one of your composites and color-grade it THREE ways: one warm (afternoon light, hopeful), one cold (blue/gray, tense), one high-contrast B&W (dramatic, noir). Use only the Tone and Color controls in FX Studio. Export all three. Write in your log: which version best matches your story's mood?

03.4 Scene #2 & #3 — Full Production Run PRODUCTION 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Students build scenes 2 and 3 of their comic story using the full FX Studio workflow. Teacher conferences with individual students on scene continuity, tone consistency, and story logic. Students begin connecting scenes into a sequence.

Student Activity

Build 2 more composited scenes today using the full workflow: background → character → FX stack → color grade. Each scene should connect to your story — think of it as your comic's middle section. When finished, line up all 3 scenes. Do they feel like they belong to the same story?

03.5 Mid-Course Review — Scene Critique & Story Check CRITIQUE 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Mid-course milestone review. Students display 3 composited scenes. Teacher and peers assess: Does the tone match? Does the character feel like they belong? Is there a visual story developing? Rubric-based assessment provided this week.

3 COMPOSITED SCENES — MID-COURSE

This is the mid-point check. You have a world, a character, and now three scenes that could be comic panels. These three scenes are the backbone of your final story.

  • 3 exported composites — character in scene with FX stack applied

04.1 Speech Bubbles & Thought Clouds — The Voice of the Page DESIGN LAB 55 min ▶
Session Summary

The anatomy of a speech bubble: shape communicates tone. Round = friendly. Jagged = shouting. Cloud = thought. Whisper lines = quiet. Students study 20 examples from different comic genres, then use pscomixx.com's bubble tool to add dialogue to their 3 composited scenes.

Student Activity

Add speech bubbles to all 3 of your composited scenes. Use at least 3 different bubble styles across the 3 scenes. The character's voice should come through in the font AND the bubble shape. Dialogue must be original — minimum 1 line per scene. Save panel versions with and without bubbles.

THE BUBBLE IS NOT DECORATION. IT'S VOICE.
04.2 SFX Text & Action Words — POW, CRASH, STATIC, GLITCH TYPE DESIGN 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Sound effects as visual design. Students study how comic SFX typography works — how the font, size, color, and angle of "CRASH" communicates the sound. Using pscomixx.online's FX text tool, students create custom SFX words for their scenes. They also explore glitch text, neon glow text, and distressed ink text styles.

Student Activity

Pick your most action-packed scene. Add a SFX word that matches the moment — don't use generic words. Make up a new one that fits YOUR story's world. (Example: In a tech world, instead of "POW" try "CRASHED" or "SIGNAL_LOST"). Style it using 2 FX text effects. Make it feel like it grew out of the scene.

04.3 Title Cards & Chapter Headers — The Cover Mindset BRAND DESIGN 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Great comics have a visual identity — a consistent title treatment, chapter header style, and color system. Students design their comic's title card: the logo-level treatment of their comic's name that will appear on the cover and chapter breaks.

Student Activity

Design your comic's title card. This is the LOGO of your story. Choose 1 display font from the FX Studio font library. Pick your 2 primary story colors. Apply at least one text effect (glow, shadow, distress, or outline). Make 3 versions — different sizes — for cover, chapter break, and social post. Save all three.

04.4 Price Tags & Promo Graphics — The Creator Economy Layer PROMO DESIGN 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Real creators sell their work. Students learn to design price tags, issue labels, promo badges, and starburst callouts — the same design language used on magazine covers, indie comics, and creator stores. Using pscomixx.online's element library, they design a full set of promo graphic assets for their comic.

Student Activity

Design a promo set for your comic's "launch." You'll build: (1) A price tag in your story colors — "$4.99 // Issue 01" (2) A launch badge — "NEW RELEASE" or "LIMITED RUN" starburst (3) A promo banner — your title card + your best scene + price tag combined. This is what you'd use if you were selling this at a zine fair or on your creator page.

IF IT'S GOOD ENOUGH TO MAKE, IT'S GOOD ENOUGH TO SELL.

05.1 Story Structure — The 3-Act Framework for Comics WRITING 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Applied narrative structure: Setup, Confrontation, Resolution — adapted for short-form comics. Students map their 3 existing scenes onto the 3-act framework, identify what's missing, and plan 3–6 additional panels needed to complete the story.

Student Activity

Map your 3 composited scenes: which is your setup? Which is your confrontation? Which (if you have it) is your resolution? Fill in the Story Map template: identify 3 gap moments — things that need to happen between your existing scenes to make the story work. These become your new panels to build this week.

05.2 Panel Layout — Rhythm, Size & Sequence LAYOUT DESIGN 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Panel size communicates time and importance. Wide panels = slow moments, epic scale. Small panels = fast cuts, rapid action. Full-page panels = the big reveal. Students study layout examples from manga, Western comics, and indie zines, then draft their page layouts using the pscomixx.com panel builder.

Student Activity

Open pscomixx.com Panel Builder. Set up your 6–12 panel layout. Don't fill content yet — just set the SIZE and SHAPE of each panel to match the pacing you want. Your biggest panel should be your most important moment. Your smallest panels should be your fastest cuts. Screenshot your blank layout. This is your storyboard.

TURN MOMENTS INTO STORIES.
05.3 Fill the Panels — Placing Scenes, Characters & Dialogue PRODUCTION 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Students drop their composited scenes into the panel layout, add dialogue from their creation logs, and shoot or build any missing gap panels. Teacher facilitates "read-through" check — students read each other's comics aloud to test if the story tracks.

Student Activity

Populate your panel layout with your scenes and dialogue. If you have gaps — panels with no scene yet — either use an existing scene at a different crop, or build a simple new composite quickly. When done, hand your device to a partner. They read your comic aloud. Do they understand the story? What confused them?

05.4 Revise & Recut — The Edit Pass EDITING 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Editing is where good stories become great stories. Students revise dialogue, reorder panels, cut panels that slow the pace, and refine the comic's ending. Focus on: Is the ending satisfying? Does every panel earn its place? Is there one moment that lands emotionally?

Student Activity

Read your comic from the top. Mark every panel where the story slows down or feels confusing. Fix those panels — rewrite dialogue, swap the scene, cut it entirely. Then read it again. Repeat until every panel pulls you forward. Write in your log: what did you cut and why?

05.5 Story Draft Review — Read-Around CRITIQUE 55 min ▶
Session Summary

Full class read-around: every student's comic draft is read by at least 2 peers. Feedback is structured: What was the strongest panel? What moment could be clearer? What would make you want to read issue 2? Students receive written feedback cards.

6–12 PANEL COMIC DRAFT

You now have a full comic draft. It's not final yet — but it has a beginning, a middle, and an end. Next week you make it publish-ready.

  • Complete comic — 6–12 panels in pscomixx.com panel builder
  • Dialogue in every panel — original writing
  • At least one full-page or splash panel
  • Peer feedback received — 2 reader response cards
  • Creator reflection: what does your story actually say?

Student Activity

Export your best panel in 3 formats: square crop, vertical crop, wide crop. For each, write a 1–2 sentence caption that makes someone want to read the full comic. Don't give away the ending. Make them click. These are real social posts — you could post them today.
